# Screenshot Test Universe

This document defines the fictional universe used across all screenshot test content. All
screenshots should be consistent with this document. When writing new content, treat this as the
source of truth for characters, projects, timelines, and tone.

## The Company

**Alpine** is a small, fast-moving software company building a productivity suite — also called
Alpine — for technical teams. The product includes collaborative documents, a task tracker, chat, a
forum, an inbox for notifications across tools, and intelligent search. The team uses Alpine itself
to run the company: planning in tasks, deciding in documents, communicating in chat and forum.

Screenshots are based on this fictional company — rather than a random or abstract scenario — so
that any member of the real Alpine team can easily read the content and check it for coherence and
correctness. An engineer, marketer, or salesperson at Alpine can all recognize whether the content
makes sense, even if they're not familiar with the specific feature being shown.

Coherence across screenshots also matters because these images are used in marketing materials,
support pages, and product documentation. Screenshots taken at different times, for different
features, should all feel like they come from the same company and the same moment in time. They
should reinforce each other, not contradict each other.

**Company stage:** ~25 people total, but our cast represents the core product team. Series A,
venture-backed. Headquartered in New York City, with team members distributed across the US.

**Voice:** Direct, collaborative, mildly irreverent. No corporate speak. People say what they mean.
Short sentences in chat. Longer, structured thinking in documents. The forum is where bigger ideas
get debated.

## Cast

### Rose Compas — CEO

Rose founded Alpine. She sets direction, manages investors, and is the face of the company
externally. She writes the vision documents that kick off every major initiative and shows up in
forum threads when something matters at a company level. In day-to-day operation she trusts her team

### Cass Cade — Chief of Staff

Cass is the operational center of gravity. She runs cross-functional coordination, manages the

formal posts.

**Typical content:** Sprint board, task collections, project tracking documents, cross-team chat

## Timeline

All screenshot content takes place within a **six-week window: September 8 – October 17, 2025.**
This is an ordinary stretch of work — the team is in their normal rhythm, no crunch, no countdown.
Two small features ship during this period but neither dominates the conversation. The energy is
steady, confident, and focused.

**Time zone:** Content timestamps use **America/New_York** (Eastern Time) unless otherwise noted.
Characters are distributed: Rose and Cass are in NYC (HQ), Matt is in NYC, Elle is in Seattle, Mason
is in Austin, Cliff is in Chicago, Holly is in Denver. Use timestamps that reflect realistic working
hours for each character's location.

**Week-by-week:**

- **Weeks 1–2 (Sep 8–19):** Normal sprint work. Elle is working on realtime reliability. Mason has
  started building tables in the rich text editor — a genuinely complex piece of work that will take
  the full period. Matt is running an editor interaction audit: before tables ships, he wants a
  complete map of every keyboard shortcut, toolbar state, and contextual behavior in the editor so
  the new feature fits into a coherent system rather than adding to existing inconsistency. He's
  also compiling the results of a customer feedback survey Holly ran at the end of August — one of
  the top requests was better table support, which is satisfying timing. Matt and Mason are already
  going back and forth on the interaction model. Holly is writing the first customer case study and
  has started work on updated sales enablement materials with Cliff — a one-pager and a short demo
  script that reflect how customers actually use Alpine now. Cliff is working three active deals and
  just got a warm referral from a happy customer.

- **Week 3 (Sep 22–26):** Mason has the core of tables working — you can insert a table, type in
  cells, navigate with Tab, add and remove rows and columns. Matt does a thorough design review,
  drawing on his interaction audit findings, and comes back with significant notes: the cell
  selection model feels wrong, the toolbar placement conflicts with the floating format menu, and he
  has strong opinions about how keyboard navigation should work between cells. Holly finishes a
  draft of the sales one-pager and sends it to Cliff for review. Elle discovers the thundering herd
  problem in her reconnection logic and posts a short technical update.

- **Week 4 (Sep 29 – Oct 3):** Mason works through most of Matt's feedback, but a debate opens up
  about column resizing. Mason implements smooth continuous resizing — drag the column border, width
  follows your cursor. Matt argues it should snap to a grid instead, so users naturally produce
  well-proportioned columns rather than tables full of arbitrary pixel widths. Mason thinks snapping
  feels restrictive and will frustrate users who have a specific layout in mind. Holly shares the
  customer survey summary with the team — key findings include demand for tables (good timing),
  notification control, and faster search on large workspaces. The survey summary feeds directly
  into Cass's Q4 planning inputs. Elle revises her reconnection approach and posts an updated plan.
  A candidate for the senior backend role Rose interviewed has a strong final-round interview and
  Cass + Rose debrief in chat.

- **Week 5 (Oct 6–10):** Cass makes the call on column resizing: snap by default, but hold Alt for
  smooth resize. Both Mason and Matt can live with it. They do a final walkthrough. Cliff has been
  using the draft one-pager in demos and has feedback for Holly — some language is too technical and
  a section on the inbox is missing. They iterate over chat. Holly's first customer case study
  publishes. Cliff gets a strong new inbound prospect through it. The senior engineer hire moves to
  offer stage.

- **Week 6 (Oct 13–17):** Tables ships. Holly writes the help doc and posts the announcement in the

  and has early thoughts he wants to get down before they disappear. Cass circulates the Q4 planning

## Projects

### 1. Realtime Reliability

**Owner:** Elle **Status:** In progress throughout the period. Completes in week 6.

**Summary:** Alpine's realtime infrastructure — the layer that powers live document co-editing, chat
message delivery, presence indicators, and task live-updates — had a minor incident the week before
this period opens: a routine deployment caused a brief but disruptive sync outage for a subset of
users. Elle wrote the postmortem and is now addressing the underlying fragility. The work involves
hardening reconnection behavior, reducing deployment blast radius, and adding better health checks
and alerting.

In week 3, Elle discovers a thundering herd problem: when a server restarts, all disconnected
clients attempt to reconnect simultaneously, briefly overwhelming the new instance before it's fully
warmed up. She posts a short technical update and revises the approach to use jittered exponential
backoff on the client side. By week 6 the full set of changes is rolled out incrementally and she
posts a brief summary.

This project is invisible to users when it works. Elle cares about it anyway.

**Key content:**

- The incident postmortem from the week before the period (written by Elle, dry and factual)
- Elle's technical design doc covering the reliability improvements
- A mid-project forum update where Elle explains the thundering herd discovery
- Tasks tracking each phase: reconnection logic, deployment health checks, alerting
- A chat thread where Mason asks how it's going and Elle explains the jitter fix in two sentences

### 2. Tables in the Rich Text Editor

**Owner:** Mason (implementation), Matt (design) **Status:** Ships in week 6 (October 15).

**Summary:** Alpine's rich text editor gains table support. This is a substantial, complex feature —
far more than it looks from the outside. Tables in a rich text editor require a custom selection
model (separate from the document selection), keyboard navigation that feels natural (Tab through
cells, arrows within a cell and move between cells at cell boundaries), and careful handling of edge
cases like pasting spreadsheet content or nesting lists inside cells.

Matt has a lot of opinions. He references the history of document layout and typesetting to argue
for specific interaction choices. He's strongly in favor of making cell selection feel "spatial"
rather than "document-like." The central debate of the project is about column resizing: Mason
implements smooth continuous resizing; Matt argues it should snap to a grid so users produce
well-proportioned tables rather than ones full of arbitrary pixel widths. Cass resolves it with a
compromise — snap by default, hold Alt for smooth resize — and both accept it.

The feature ships in week 6. Holly writes the help doc and announces it in the forum.

**Key content:**

- Matt's design spec, with sections on selection model, toolbar placement, and column resizing
- Mason's implementation task collection, with tasks for each subsystem (selection, keyboard nav,
  toolbar, column resizing) and notes on what was tricky
- The column resizing debate: a chat thread or task comment where Matt argues for snapping, Mason
  argues for smooth, and Cass's compromise lands cleanly
- Holly's forum announcement and help doc when it ships

### 3. Editor Interaction Audit

**Owner:** Matt **Status:** In progress weeks 1–3. Referenced throughout the tables project.

**Summary:** Before tables ships, Matt wants a complete, honest map of the Alpine editor's current
interaction model — every keyboard shortcut, every toolbar state, every contextual behavior, every

Matt writes this up as a living document. It's part reference, part design critique. Some of what he
finds is fine; some of it he flags as debt to address. The tables design review in week 3 draws
directly on the audit's findings — several of his notes reference specific inconsistencies he
documented there.

**Key content:**

- Matt's audit document: a structured inventory of editor interactions, annotated with "works well",
  "acceptable", and "needs revisiting" verdicts, with first-principles reasoning for each

## Content Guidelines

### Tone

- **Chat messages:** Short. 1–3 sentences. Natural speech patterns. People do not write in complete
  formal sentences in chat.
- **Document content:** Structured with headers. Purposeful and specific. No filler content.
  Engineers write dense, technical prose. Holly writes clearly for a mixed audience.
- **Forum posts:** More considered than chat. The author has thought about what they want to say.
  Threads can get a few replies deep.

### What to Avoid

- **Corporate speak.** No "synergize", "leverage", "circle back", "touch base", "move the needle."
- **Vague content.** "Working on some things" is not interesting. Content should be specific.
  Feature names, metrics, real decisions.
- **Unrealistic technical language.** Don't make up convincing-sounding but wrong technical details.
  Keep technical content high-level or accurately grounded.
- **Inconsistency with the timeline.** If a task is marked as shipped on Jan 20, chat content from
  Jan 22 should not treat it as still in progress.
- **All-caps or heavy emoji in business content.** One or two emoji in a casual chat message is
  fine. Not in documents.

### Perspective

Most screenshots are taken from **Cass Cade's perspective** (logged in as Cass). This means:

- The left sidebar shows Cass's spaces and navigation
- DM conversations show Cass's messages on the right
- Notifications are Cass's notifications
- The task board may be filtered to Cass's assigned items or team-wide views

When screenshots are from another character's perspective, it should be for a specific reason (e.g.,
showing Matt's design review document, which he owns).
